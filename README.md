# Git Practice — Software Development Article

## Article

Joel Spolsky, December 25, 2001. *Getting Things Done When You're Only a Grunt*. Joel on Software. [Read the article here](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2001/12/25/getting-things-done-when-youre-only-a-grunt/).

## Why I Found It Interesting

Spolsky's angle is less about being professional, and more about wielding influence without formal authority. His core claim is that you don't need to be a manager to fix a broken process, you just start doing the process yourself, quietly, and let its usefulness pull other people in. He gives concrete examples: building your own bug database and log tickets against coworkers' code, nagging them by email until they cave and start using it themselves. Keeping your own source control checkouts until people come to you for help. Writing your own specs and schedules even if nobody asked. It's less "be a good citizen," and more "engineer social pressure towards the outcome you want."

What stood out to me is how tactical (even if a little sly) some of the advice gets. His strategy for dealing with a genuinely poor programmer isn't to mentor them or fix their code, it's to keep filing bugs against their work so they stay "neutralized" on one contained piece of the system, instead of doing damage elsewhere. Similarly, his advice on handling promising-but-inexperienced coworkers isn't direct criticism, it's letting them discover their own bugs so the lesson sticks without making them defensive. 

## Key Takeaway

My main takeaway is that you can improve a team from the bottom, but only if you're clearly excellent at your actual job first. He's explicit that trying to fix the process when you're not already pulling your weight just reads as annoying, and gets you resented. The actual strategies he uses, though, are influence-by-example, plus a bit of quiet manipulation of incentives (email nags, controlled bug-hunts, controlled checkin frequency, etc.). Not simply "be diligent and communicate well."
